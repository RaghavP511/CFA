LM1 · The Term Structure and Interest Rate Dynamics

LOS: (a) spot, forward, YTM, expected and realized returns and curve shape; (b) bootstrapping spot rates from the par curve; (c) evolution of spot rates implied by forwards; (d) rolling down the yield curve; (e) the swap rate curve; (f, g) spreads; (h) term structure theories; (i) yield curve factors and (j) key rate duration.
Discount factor from a spot rate
\[ DF_T=\frac{1}{(1+z_T)^{T}} \]
\(z_T\) is the annualized \(T\)-year spot rate on an option-free, default-risk-free zero.
Forward rate model
\[ (1+z_{A+B})^{A+B}=(1+z_A)^{A}\,\bigl(1+f_{A,B}\bigr)^{B} \]
\(f_{A,B}\) = rate agreed today on a \(B\)-year loan starting in \(A\) years. Read the subscripts as "start, tenor".
Forward rate, solved
\[ f_{A,B}=\left[\frac{(1+z_{A+B})^{A+B}}{(1+z_A)^{A}}\right]^{1/B}-1 \]
Forward price of a zero (forward pricing model)
\[ P_{A,B}=\frac{P_{A+B}}{P_A} \]
\(P_T\) is today's price of a \(T\)-year zero per 1 of par.
Bootstrapping the next spot rate
\[ 100=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{C}{(1+z_t)^t}+\frac{100+C}{(1+z_T)^{T}} \]
Par bond, so price = 100 and \(C\) = the par rate. Solve forward one maturity at a time.
Rolling down the yield curve
\[ r_{\text{roll}}=\underbrace{\text{coupon yield}}_{\text{carry}}+\underbrace{\frac{P_{\text{end}}-P_{\text{begin}}}{P_{\text{begin}}}}_{\text{price effect of aging into a lower yield}} \]
Works only if the curve is upward sloping and does not shift.
Swap spread
\[ \text{Swap spread}= \text{swap fixed rate}-\text{government YTM (same maturity)} \]
I-spread, Z-spread, TED, Libor–OIS
\[ \text{I-spread}=\text{bond YTM}-\text{swap rate};\qquad P=\sum_{t}\frac{CF_t}{(1+z_t+Z)^{t}} \]
The Z-spread is the constant add-on to every spot rate that makes discounted cash flows equal price. TED = 3M Libor − 3M T-bill; Libor–OIS = 3M Libor − OIS rate. Both are money-market stress gauges.
Key rate duration
\[ \% \Delta P \approx -\sum_{i} \text{KeyRateDur}_i \times \Delta z_i \]
Effective duration answers a parallel shift; key rate durations answer a shift at one maturity.
Three-factor decomposition of curve moves
\[ \Delta \text{curve}\approx \underbrace{L}_{\text{level}}+\underbrace{S}_{\text{steepness}}+\underbrace{C}_{\text{curvature}} \]
Level explains the large majority of the variance in curve changes.

Part 1 — Spot, forward and the model that links them (LOS a, b)

Everything in this module hangs off one identity: investing for \(A+B\) years at the spot rate must equal investing for \(A\) years at the spot rate and then rolling into a forward contract for \(B\) years. If it did not, you could arbitrage the difference.

\[ (1+z_{A+B})^{A+B}=(1+z_A)^{A}(1+f_{A,B})^{B} \]

Read the subscripts carefully, because this is where marks are lost. \(f_{2,1}\) is a one-year rate starting two years from now. \(f_{1,2}\) is a two-year rate starting one year from now. Same three-year horizon, different animals.

Bootstrapping goes the other way: from observable par rates to unobservable spot rates. Take the one-year par rate as the one-year spot. Then price the two-year par bond at 100, discount the first coupon at the known one-year spot, and solve for the two-year spot that makes the remainder work. Repeat.

Part 2 — YTM is not a return (LOS c)

YTM is only the realized return if three things hold: you hold to maturity, the issuer makes every payment, and you reinvest every coupon at the YTM. A zero-coupon bond satisfies the third by construction, which is exactly why the spot curve is built from zeros.

On an upward-sloping curve, YTM sits below the longest spot rate, because it is a blended discount rate across the whole cash flow stream.

Part 3 — Rolling down the yield curve (LOS d)

If the curve is upward sloping and stays put, a bond ages into a lower yield. Its price rises toward par from below the horizon yield, and that price gain sits on top of the coupon. Buy longer than your horizon, sell before maturity, capture the roll.

Trap: the strategy depends on the curve not shifting. It is a bet on a stable, upward-sloping curve, not a free lunch. A parallel upward shift can wipe out the roll entirely.

Part 4 — The swap rate curve (LOS e)

Why use swaps as the benchmark rather than governments? Swap rates reflect private-sector credit, the market is deep at many maturities, there is no on-the-run/off-the-run distortion, and no government has to issue at that maturity for the rate to exist. Banks in particular hedge in swaps, so swaps are their natural benchmark. Investors who own government bonds outright tend to prefer the government curve.

The swap spread is the swap fixed rate minus the government yield of the same maturity. It widens when credit or funding conditions deteriorate.

Part 5 — Spreads (LOS f, g)

The G-spread and I-spread each compare one yield to one benchmark yield, which embeds whatever distortion sits in that single maturity. The Z-spread avoids the problem by adding a constant to every spot rate and solving for the constant that reproduces the market price. That makes it the cleaner measure when the curve is steep.

Part 6 — Theories and factors (LOS h, i, j)

The theories differ in one question: do forward rates contain a risk premium, and if so, does it grow with maturity? Pure expectations says no premium. Liquidity preference says yes and it grows. Preferred habitat says yes but it need not grow monotonically, because the premium comes from supply and demand imbalances in each maturity bucket.

Empirically, curve movements decompose into level, steepness and curvature, with level dominating. Effective duration captures only a parallel shift, which is why key rate durations exist: they tell you the exposure to a move at one specific point on the curve, and they sum to approximately the effective duration.

Why does \(f_{1,2}\) not mean "the forward rate in two years for one year"?
The first subscript is always the start date and the second is always the tenor. \(f_{1,2}\) starts in one year and lasts two years. If a vignette gives you \(z_1\) and \(z_3\), the rate you can back out is \(f_{1,2}\), not \(f_{2,1}\). Getting this backwards produces a number that looks plausible and is wrong.
If the curve is upward sloping, does that mean rates are expected to rise?
Only under pure expectations. Under liquidity preference the upward slope can be entirely a term premium with flat expected rates. The exam likes to ask what an upward slope "implies" and the answer depends on which theory you are told to assume.
When is the Z-spread better than the G-spread?
When the curve is steep or the bond's cash flows are spread across many maturities. The G-spread compares to a single point on the curve, so a steep curve makes it maturity-dependent. The Z-spread uses the whole curve, so it is not distorted by which single benchmark you picked.
Do key rate durations replace effective duration?
No, they decompose it. Effective duration answers "what if the whole curve moves 1%". Key rate durations answer "what if only the 5-year point moves". They sum to roughly the effective duration, and they are the tool for non-parallel shifts.
Can rolling down the curve lose money?
Yes. The roll return assumes the curve is unchanged at the horizon. If rates rise, the price loss can exceed the coupon plus roll. It is a positive-carry trade with duration risk attached, not an arbitrage.
1. Spot rates are \(z_1=4\%\), \(z_2=5\%\) and \(z_3=6\%\), annually compounded. The one-year forward rate two years forward, \(f_{2,1}\), is closest to:
  1. 6.0%
  2. 7.0%
  3. 8.0%
Answer
C is correct. \[ f_{2,1}=\frac{(1.06)^3}{(1.05)^2}-1=\frac{1.191016}{1.1025}-1=0.0803 \] Roughly 8.03%. A is the three-year spot rate itself. B comes from using \((1.06)^3/(1.04)^1\) style mismatched exponents.
2. An analyst observes an upward-sloping, stable yield curve and buys a 5-year bond intending to sell it in one year. Absent any curve shift, the expected one-year return will most likely be:
  1. equal to the bond's YTM
  2. greater than the bond's YTM
  3. equal to the one-year spot rate
Answer
B is correct. The bond ages from a 5-year yield to a 4-year yield, which on an upward-sloping curve is lower, so the price rises. That roll-down price gain is added to the coupon, producing a return above YTM. A would hold only on a flat curve. C is what pure expectations implies for a risk-neutral world with no roll.
3. A bond trades at a G-spread of 120 bps and a Z-spread of 145 bps. The difference is best explained by:
  1. the bond's embedded option
  2. the shape of the spot curve
  3. a deterioration in the issuer's credit
Answer
B is correct. The G-spread compares the bond's YTM to a single government yield; the Z-spread is applied to every spot rate. When the curve is not flat the two diverge, and the gap widens with the steepness of the curve. A describes the OAS-versus-Z-spread difference, not this one. C would move both spreads together.
4. Which theory is most consistent with a term premium that does not increase monotonically with maturity?
  1. Liquidity preference theory
  2. Pure expectations theory
  3. Preferred habitat theory
Answer
C is correct. Under preferred habitat the premium reflects supply and demand imbalances in each maturity bucket, so it can be larger at 7 years than at 10. A requires the premium to grow with maturity. B has no premium at all.
5. A portfolio has an effective duration of 6.0. Its 2-year key rate duration is 1.0, its 5-year is 2.5 and its 10-year is 2.5. If only the 10-year spot rate rises by 25 bps, the portfolio value change is closest to:
  1. −0.63%
  2. −1.50%
  3. −0.25%
Answer
A is correct. \(-2.5\times0.0025=-0.00625\), about −0.63%. B applies the full effective duration, which is only right for a parallel shift. C ignores the duration entirely.

LM2 · The Arbitrage-Free Valuation Framework

LOS: (a) what arbitrage-free valuation means; (b) value an option-free coupon bond; (c, d) the binomial interest rate tree and its calibration; (e) backward induction; (f) lattice versus zero curve; (g) pathwise valuation; (h) Monte Carlo forward-rate simulation; (i) term structure models.
Arbitrage-free value of an option-free bond
\[ V_0=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{CF_t}{(1+z_t)^{t}} \]
Every cash flow discounted at its own spot rate. Using one YTM for all of them is the shortcut that creates the arbitrage.
Backward induction at a node
\[ V_{\text{node}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{V_{u}+C_{u}}{1+i_{\text{node}}}+\frac{V_{d}+C_{d}}{1+i_{\text{node}}}\right] \]
Risk-neutral probabilities are 50/50 by construction in the standard lognormal tree. Discount at the rate at that node, not the forward.
Lognormal spacing of rates within a period
\[ i_{u}=i_{d}\,e^{2\sigma},\qquad\text{more generally}\qquad i_{n,j}=i_{n,0}\,e^{2j\sigma} \]
\(\sigma\) is the assumed interest rate volatility per period. Lognormality keeps rates positive and makes higher rates more volatile in absolute terms.
Calibration condition
\[ \text{Tree value of each benchmark par bond}=100 \]
Solve period by period for the rate level that reproduces the benchmark curve, given \(\sigma\). This is what makes the tree arbitrage-free.
Pathwise valuation
\[ V_0=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}V_p,\qquad N=2^{\,n-1} \]
\(n\) periods gives \(2^{n-1}\) paths. Average the path values; the answer matches backward induction exactly.
Monte Carlo with a drift adjustment
\[ V_0=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t}\frac{CF_{t,k}}{\prod_{s\le t}\bigl(1+r_{s,k}+\delta\bigr)} \]
\(\delta\) is the constant added to every simulated rate so the model reprices the benchmark bonds. Without it the simulation is not arbitrage-free.
The one idea
Two portfolios with identical future cash flows must have the same price today. Value of the whole = sum of the values of the parts. If a coupon bond's price differs from the sum of its stripped cash flows valued at spot rates, you can strip or reconstitute and take the difference risk free.

Equilibrium versus arbitrage-free: equilibrium models start from economic assumptions and produce a curve that generally will not match the market. Arbitrage-free models take today's curve as given and fit it exactly. For pricing a specific bond against the market, you want arbitrage-free.

Part 1 — What "arbitrage-free" actually means (LOS a, b)

Two arbitrage types matter here. Value additivity: the whole must equal the sum of the parts, which is what strip-and-reconstitute trades enforce in the Treasury market. Dominance: a risk-free asset cannot offer a higher return than another risk-free asset. Violate either and there is a free lunch.

The practical consequence for LOS b: discount each coupon at the spot rate matching its own date, not at a single YTM. On a sloped curve the two answers differ, and the spot-rate answer is the arbitrage-free one.

Part 2 — Why we need a tree at all (LOS c)

The spot curve prices fixed cash flows. It cannot price a bond whose cash flows depend on where rates go, because there is no single future to discount. The binomial tree fixes this by giving rates a set of future states with probabilities attached.

Construction: at each node, the up rate and down rate are separated by \(e^{2\sigma}\), with equal 50% risk-neutral probabilities. Lognormal spacing keeps rates positive and makes absolute volatility rise with the rate level, which matches how rates actually behave.

Part 3 — Calibration is the whole game (LOS d)

An uncalibrated tree is just a picture. Calibration forces it to reprice the benchmark curve: you choose the rate level at each period so that valuing each benchmark par bond through the tree returns exactly 100. Volatility \(\sigma\) is an input; the rate levels are the unknowns you solve for.

Once calibrated, the tree is arbitrage-free with respect to today's curve. Any bond you then value through it is priced consistently with the market.

Part 4 — Backward induction (LOS e)

Start at maturity where the value is known, work leftward. At each node take the expected value of the two next-period outcomes including their cash flows, discount one period at the rate at that node. Two errors cost marks: discounting at the wrong node's rate, and forgetting to add the coupon before discounting.

Part 5 — Pathwise and Monte Carlo (LOS g, h)

Pathwise valuation enumerates every route through the tree, \(2^{n-1}\) of them for \(n\) periods, values the bond along each path, and averages. It gives the same answer as backward induction for path-independent bonds, and it makes the mechanics obvious.

Monte Carlo exists for one reason: path dependency. A mortgage-backed security's prepayments depend on the path rates took to get here, not just on today's rate. A lattice cannot represent that; a simulation of thousands of forward-rate paths can. The simulation must be forced to reprice the benchmarks, which is done by adding a constant drift term \(\delta\) to every simulated rate until it does.

Trap: more Monte Carlo paths reduce statistical noise. They do not fix a wrong model or wrong prepayment assumptions. Precision is not accuracy.

Part 6 — Term structure models (LOS i)

Equilibrium models (CIR, Vasicek) derive the curve from assumptions about the short rate process and the economy. They are parsimonious but will not match the observed curve. Arbitrage-free models (Ho–Lee, KWF) start from the observed curve and fit it exactly, which is what you need for relative-value pricing. CIR's \(\sqrt{r}\) volatility term keeps rates non-negative; Vasicek's constant volatility does not.

If backward induction and pathwise valuation give the same answer, why learn both?
They only agree for path-independent securities. The moment cash flows depend on the route taken - MBS prepayments being the standard case - pathwise thinking generalises to Monte Carlo and backward induction does not. The exam also tests the mechanics of both directly.
Where does the 50/50 probability come from? Real rate moves aren't coin flips.
They are risk-neutral probabilities, not real-world ones. The tree is calibrated so that using 50/50 reproduces observed market prices. All the information about the real-world drift is absorbed into the calibrated rate levels.
Does raising the volatility assumption change the value of an option-free bond?
No. Recalibrating with a higher \(\sigma\) spreads the rates out but the tree still reprices the benchmarks exactly, so an option-free bond's value is unchanged. Volatility only matters once there is an option in the bond - which is LM3.
Why can't Monte Carlo just be run without the drift adjustment?
Because an unadjusted simulation will not reprice the benchmark bonds, which means it is not arbitrage-free and any relative-value conclusion from it is unreliable. The constant \(\delta\) is added to every simulated rate until the model reproduces market prices.
Which is "better", an equilibrium or an arbitrage-free model?
Neither in the abstract. Arbitrage-free models fit today's curve exactly, which is what you want to price a bond relative to the market. Equilibrium models have fewer parameters and an economic story, which is what you want for scenario work and long-horizon analysis.

  1. 100.00
  2. 100.47
  3. 101.30
Answer
B is correct. 2. An analyst values an option-free bond two ways: discounting each cash flow at its spot rate, and backward induction through a correctly calibrated binomial tree. The two values will most likely be:
  1. equal
  2. different, with the tree value higher
  3. different, with the tree value lower
Answer
A is correct. Calibration forces the tree to reproduce the benchmark curve, so for a security with fixed, path-independent cash flows both methods must return the same arbitrage-free value. A difference would signal a calibration error.
3. A binomial tree covers four annual periods. The number of distinct interest rate paths is:
  1. 4
  2. 8
  3. 16
Answer
B is correct. \(2^{n-1}=2^{3}=8\). C is \(2^n\), the common slip.
4. Which security most likely requires Monte Carlo simulation rather than a binomial lattice?
  1. A callable corporate bond
  2. A mortgage-backed security
  3. A putable corporate bond
Answer
B is correct. MBS cash flows are path dependent: prepayment behaviour depends on the history of rates, including burnout, not just the current rate. A lattice node carries no memory of how it was reached. A and C are path independent and are handled fine by the lattice.
5. Which model is an equilibrium term structure model in which the short rate cannot become negative?
  1. Vasicek
  2. Cox–Ingersoll–Ross
  3. Ho–Lee
Answer
B is correct. CIR's volatility term scales with \(\sqrt{r}\), which collapses to zero as the rate approaches zero. A is an equilibrium model but its constant volatility permits negative rates. C is arbitrage-free, not equilibrium.

LM3 · Valuation and Analysis of Bonds with Embedded Options

LOS: (a, b) embedded options and the value relationships; (c, f) valuation on the arbitrage-free tree; (d) volatility effects; (e) level and shape of the curve; (g, h) option-adjusted spread; (i, j) effective duration; (k) one-sided and key rate durations; (l) effective convexity; (m) capped and floored floaters; (n, o) convertibles.
The two value identities
\[ V_{\text{callable}}=V_{\text{straight}}-V_{\text{call}} \] \[ V_{\text{putable}}=V_{\text{straight}}+V_{\text{put}} \]
The call belongs to the issuer, so it is subtracted from the investor's value. The put belongs to the investor, so it is added.
Option value backed out of the tree
\[ V_{\text{call}}=V_{\text{straight}}-V_{\text{callable}},\qquad V_{\text{put}}=V_{\text{putable}}-V_{\text{straight}} \]
Node rule with a call
\[ V_{\text{node}}=\min\!\left[\text{call price},\ \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{V_u+C}{1+i}+\tfrac{V_d+C}{1+i}\right)\right] \]
Putable bonds use \(\max[\text{put price},\ \cdot]\). Apply the rule after discounting, at every node where the option is exercisable.
Option-adjusted spread
\[ \text{OAS}= \text{Z-spread}-\text{option cost};\qquad \text{option cost}=\text{Z-spread}-\text{OAS} \]
OAS is the constant spread added to every rate in the tree that makes the model value equal the market price. It is the compensation for credit and liquidity with the option stripped out, so it is the only spread that compares a callable to an option-free bond fairly.
Effective duration and convexity
\[ \text{EffDur}=\frac{PV_{-}-PV_{+}}{2\times PV_{0}\times \Delta \text{curve}} \] \[ \text{EffCon}=\frac{PV_{-}+PV_{+}-2PV_{0}}{PV_{0}\times(\Delta \text{curve})^{2}} \]
The curve is shifted, the tree is recalibrated, and the bond is revalued. Modified duration is meaningless here because the cash flows change with rates.
One-sided durations
\[ \text{Dur}^{-}=\frac{PV_{-}-PV_{0}}{PV_0\,\Delta},\qquad \text{Dur}^{+}=\frac{PV_{0}-PV_{+}}{PV_0\,\Delta} \]
For a callable trading near its call price, \(\text{Dur}^{+} > \text{Dur}^{-}\): it falls more when rates rise than it rises when rates fall.
Capped and floored floaters
\[ V_{\text{capped FRN}}=V_{\text{straight FRN}}-V_{\text{cap}},\qquad V_{\text{floored FRN}}=V_{\text{straight FRN}}+V_{\text{floor}} \]
The cap is a benefit to the issuer, the floor a benefit to the investor. Same logic as call and put.

Volatility and OAS: raise the assumed volatility and the model says the embedded call is worth more, so the model price of a callable falls; to force the model back to the observed market price, the computed OAS must fall. Higher assumed volatility → lower OAS for a callable, higher OAS for a putable. Pick the wrong volatility and you will label a bond cheap when it is not.

Part 1 — Who owns the option (LOS a, b)

Start every question by asking who holds the option. The issuer holds the call and will use it when rates fall, refinancing cheaply, which caps the investor's upside. The investor holds the put and will use it when rates rise, putting the bond back at par, which floors the downside. From there the two identities follow directly.

Part 2 — Valuing on the tree (LOS c, f)

Use the same calibrated tree from LM2, with one addition: at every node where the option is live, apply the exercise rule after discounting. Callable takes the minimum of the call price and the computed value; putable takes the maximum of the put price and the computed value. Then carry that value leftward.

Get the option value by valuing the bond twice - once ignoring the option, once applying it - and taking the difference. This is faster and less error-prone than trying to value the option directly.

Part 3 — Volatility (LOS d, h)

Higher volatility widens the tree. Wider trees make options more valuable, because an option pays off in the good tail and is protected in the bad one. Callable value falls, putable value rises.

The OAS consequence trips people up. OAS is solved for so the model matches the market price, which does not move when you change your assumption. If higher volatility lowers the model value of a callable, less spread is needed to reconcile it, so the computed OAS falls. This is why the volatility assumption has to be defensible before you call a bond cheap or rich.

Part 4 — Curve level and shape (LOS e)

As rates fall, a callable bond's price stops keeping up: it compresses toward the call price. That is price compression, and it is what makes effective duration shrink and effective convexity go negative. A putable bond behaves the other way, supported from below as rates rise.

Shape matters too. A flatter curve, all else equal, raises the value of the call option (the issuer's refinancing option becomes more likely to be in the money across the horizon) and so lowers the callable bond's value.

Part 5 — Duration and convexity (LOS i, j, k, l)

Modified duration assumes fixed cash flows, which is false here, so effective duration is the only correct measure: shift the curve, recalibrate the tree, revalue. Both callable and putable durations are lower than the straight bond's, because the option truncates part of the price response.

One-sided durations exist because the response is asymmetric. For a callable near its call price, the up-rate duration exceeds the down-rate duration. Key rate durations then show where on the curve the sensitivity sits, which for a callable clusters around the call date rather than maturity.

Part 6 — Capped and floored floaters, and convertibles (LOS m, n, o)

A capped floater is a floater minus a cap; a floored floater is a floater plus a floor. Value them on the tree by applying the cap or floor to the coupon at each node before discounting.

A convertible is a straight bond plus a call option on the issuer's stock. Its floor is the higher of the straight value and the conversion value, and it will trade above that floor because the option has time value. Watch the direction of the adjustments: an issuer call reduces the value, an investor put increases it. When the share price is far above the conversion price the convertible tracks the equity; far below, it trades like a straight bond.

Why is a callable bond's convexity negative at low rates but positive at high rates?
At high rates the call is far out of the money and the bond behaves like a straight bond, which has positive convexity. At low rates the call is live and the price is pinned near the call price, so further rate falls stop producing price gains. That flattening of the price-yield curve is negative convexity.
Is a lower OAS always a worse deal?
Compare OAS to a peer group of similar credit and liquidity. Below the peer group means the bond is expensive; above means cheap. But OAS is model output: it depends on your volatility assumption and your tree. Two analysts with different volatility assumptions get different OAS for the same bond at the same price.
Why not just use Z-spread for a callable bond?
Because the Z-spread includes the compensation the investor demands for having written the call. Comparing a callable's Z-spread to an option-free bond's Z-spread makes the callable look generous when it is really just riskier in a different way. OAS strips the option out so the comparison is like for like.
Does raising volatility change an option-free bond's OAS?
No. With no option there is nothing for volatility to act on, so the tree still reproduces the same value and OAS equals the Z-spread regardless of the volatility input.
A convertible's conversion value is below its straight value. What is it trading on?
The bond floor. It will behave largely like a straight bond, with its price driven by rates and credit rather than the share price, though it still holds some equity option value which is why it trades above the straight value.
1. A straight bond is valued at 102.50 and an otherwise identical callable bond at 99.75. The value of the embedded call option is closest to:
  1. 2.75
  2. −2.75
  3. 99.75
Answer
A is correct. \(V_{\text{call}}=V_{\text{straight}}-V_{\text{callable}}=102.50-99.75=2.75\). The option has positive value; it is subtracted from the investor's bond value, which is a different thing from being negative.
2. An analyst increases the interest rate volatility assumption from 10% to 20% when valuing a callable bond whose market price is unchanged. The computed OAS will most likely:
  1. increase
  2. decrease
  3. be unchanged
Answer
B is correct. Higher volatility makes the embedded call more valuable, lowering the model value of the callable bond. Since the market price is fixed, less spread is needed to reconcile model to market, so OAS falls. A is the putable answer. C is the option-free answer.
3. A putable bond, a callable bond and an otherwise identical straight bond are compared at a low level of interest rates. The bond with the highest effective duration is most likely the:
  1. callable bond
  2. putable bond
  3. straight bond
Answer
C is correct. Both embedded options truncate the price response, so both option-embedded bonds have effective durations at or below the straight bond's. At low rates the call is live, so the callable's duration is compressed the most. A is the lowest, not the highest.
4. A convertible bond trades at 1,180. Its conversion ratio is 20 and the underlying share trades at 52. The market conversion premium per share is closest to:
  1. 7.00
  2. 59.00
  3. 140.00
Answer
A is correct. Market conversion price \(=1{,}180/20=59\). Premium per share \(=59-52=7\). B is the market conversion price itself. C is the total premium across all 20 shares.
5. A capped floating-rate note is best described as:
  1. a straight floater plus a cap held by the investor
  2. a straight floater minus a cap held by the issuer
  3. a straight floater plus a floor held by the issuer
Answer
B is correct. The cap protects the issuer from paying above a ceiling, so it has value to the issuer and is subtracted from the investor's value: \(V_{\text{capped}}=V_{\text{straight}}-V_{\text{cap}}\). A floor works the other way and is added.

LM4 · Credit Analysis Models

LOS: expected exposure, LGD, POD and CVA; credit scores versus credit ratings and their limitations; the credit risk of a bond using its expected exposures and recovery rate; credit spread analysis and term structure; structural versus reduced-form models; credit analysis of securitised debt.
The core chain
\[ \text{LGD}=\text{Exposure}\times(1-\text{Recovery Rate}) \] \[ \text{Expected loss}=\text{LGD}\times\text{POD} \] \[ \text{CVA}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\text{Expected loss}_t}{(1+r_t)^{t}} \]
CVA is the present value of expected losses over the life of the bond. Discount at the risk-free rate, using each period's own rate.
Fair value of a risky bond
\[ V_{\text{risky}}=V_{\text{risk-free}}-\text{CVA} \]
Value it as if default-free first, then subtract the credit charge. Everything in the module hangs off this line.
Hazard rate and survival
\[ \text{POS}_t=(1-\lambda)^{t},\qquad \text{POD}_t=\lambda(1-\lambda)^{t-1} \]
\(\lambda\) is the conditional (hazard) rate of default in a period given survival to the start of it. The unconditional POD for period \(t\) falls over time even with a constant hazard rate, because survival to reach period \(t\) becomes less likely.
Credit spread, approximated
\[ \text{Credit spread}\approx \text{POD}\times\text{LGD rate}=\lambda\times(1-\text{RR}) \]
A quick sanity check only. It ignores liquidity, taxes and the risk premium investors demand above the pure expected loss.
Structural model, the option view
\[ \text{Equity}=\max(0,\ A_T-K),\qquad \text{Debt}=A_T-\max(0,\ A_T-K) \]
\(A_T\) is asset value at debt maturity, \(K\) the face value. Equity is a call on the firm's assets; owning the risky debt is owning the assets and having written a call, equivalently owning risk-free debt and having written a put with strike \(K\).
Expected exposure
\[ EE_t=\sum_{\text{nodes}} p_{\text{node}}\times(\text{bond value}_{\text{node}}+\text{coupon}) \]
Exposure is what you would lose before recovery, so it is the value of the remaining cash flows at that date, not the face amount.

Credit scores versus credit ratings
Credit scores apply to individuals and small businesses and are ordinal - a higher score is better, but the gap between 700 and 750 has no fixed meaning. Credit ratings apply to companies and their debt issues, and are also ordinal. Shared limitations: they are stable by design so they lag the market, they do not adjust for the business cycle, they say nothing about price (a highly rated bond can still be a bad buy), and event risk such as an LBO or a lawsuit is largely unpredictable.

Part 1 — Build the credit charge one step at a time

Every credit valuation question is the same four steps. Compute the expected exposure at each date. Apply the recovery rate to get LGD. Multiply by that period's probability of default to get expected loss. Discount all the expected losses back and sum them: that is CVA. Subtract CVA from the risk-free value to get the fair value of the risky bond.

The most common error is using par as the exposure. Exposure is the value of what you still have coming: the remaining coupons and principal, valued at that date. Early in the life of a long bond that can be well above or below par.

Part 2 — Hazard rates and why POD declines

The hazard rate \(\lambda\) is conditional: the chance of defaulting in this period given you made it to the start of it. The unconditional probability of defaulting in period \(t\) is \(\lambda(1-\lambda)^{t-1}\), which falls as \(t\) rises even though \(\lambda\) is constant, because you must survive to get there. Cumulative default probability, by contrast, rises with time. Questions often test whether you can keep those two straight.

Part 3 — The credit spread and what it really pays for

To a first approximation the spread compensates for expected loss, \(\lambda\times(1-\text{RR})\). In practice observed spreads are wider than expected loss alone, because investors also demand payment for liquidity, for the uncertainty in the loss estimate, and for the fact that defaults cluster in bad states of the world when losses hurt most.

The spread also moves with things that have nothing to do with the issuer: a market-wide flight to quality widens spreads across every name at once.

Part 4 — Structural models

The insight is that equity is a call option on the firm's assets with strike equal to the debt's face value. Shareholders have limited liability, so if assets are worth less than the debt at maturity they walk away and the debtholders take the assets. This means risky debt equals a risk-free bond plus a short put on the assets, and option pricing machinery then gives a probability of default and a credit spread.

The catch is practical: asset value and asset volatility are not directly observable and must be inferred from equity prices, and the model assumes a single zero-coupon debt issue, which no real firm has.

Part 5 — Reduced-form models

These skip the economic story and estimate default statistically. Default arrives as a jump process with a hazard rate estimated from observable variables, which can include the state of the economy, so the model can let default probability rise in a recession. Everything it uses is observable, which is the main advantage. The main criticism is that it presumes the historical relationships continue to hold, and it offers no explanation of why a firm defaults.

Part 6 — Securitised debt

Analysing a securitisation is a different exercise from analysing a corporate. Look at the collateral pool's composition, granularity and homogeneity; a granular, homogeneous pool of many small similar loans can be analysed statistically, while a concentrated pool of a few large exposures needs loan-by-loan work. Then look at the structure: tranching, subordination, overcollateralisation, excess spread and the servicer's quality and incentives. Finally, the sponsor: a securitisation is only as good as the origination standards behind it, and a weak sponsor with no skin in the game is a warning sign regardless of the tranche's rating.

Why does the unconditional probability of default fall over time if the hazard rate is constant?
Because to default in period 5 the issuer must first survive periods 1 through 4. That survival requirement gets harder to satisfy, so \(\lambda(1-\lambda)^{t-1}\) shrinks. Cumulative default probability still rises; only the per-period unconditional probability falls.
Should exposure ever be higher than par?
Yes. If rates have fallen or the bond carries a high coupon, the remaining cash flows are worth more than par, so exposure exceeds par. Exposure is a market value of what remains, not a face amount.
If ratings are so limited, why does anyone use them?
They are a cheap, comparable, widely understood ordinal ranking of default risk, they change slowly which is useful for mandates and covenants, and they map to a large body of historical default data. The limitations are about what they do not do: they do not price the bond, they do not adjust for the cycle, and they cannot see event risk.
Which model would you use in practice?
Reduced-form models dominate practical credit risk work because their inputs are observable and they handle the credit cycle. Structural models are the better teaching device and are still used where a firm's asset value can be estimated reasonably, notably in commercial credit models built off equity market data.
Why is a high-yield issuer's credit spread curve often downward sloping?
Its problems are immediate. The market prices a high chance of default in the next year or two; conditional on surviving that, the firm has probably refinanced or recovered, so longer-dated risk per year is lower. That inverts the curve.
1. A bond has an expected exposure of 104.00 at a given date and a recovery rate of 40%. The probability of default in that period is 1.5%. Expected loss for that period is closest to:
  1. 0.62
  2. 0.94
  3. 1.56
Answer
B is correct. \( \text{LGD}=104\times(1-0.40)=62.40\); \( \text{EL}=62.40\times0.015=0.936\). A uses the recovery rate as the loss rate the wrong way round in a different combination; C forgets recovery entirely (\(104\times0.015\)).
2. A five-year bond's risk-free value is 98.75 and its CVA is 2.10. Its fair value is closest to:
  1. 96.65
  2. 98.75
  3. 100.85
Answer
A is correct. \(V_{\text{risky}}=98.75-2.10=96.65\). CVA is the present value of expected credit losses and is always subtracted.
3. The hazard rate for an issuer is a constant 2% per year. The probability that the issuer defaults during year 3 is closest to:
  1. 1.92%
  2. 2.00%
  3. 5.88%
Answer
A is correct. \( \text{POD}_3=0.02\times(0.98)^{2}=0.0192\). B is the conditional hazard rate, not the unconditional probability. C is roughly the cumulative three-year probability.
4. Which statement best describes a limitation specific to structural credit models?
  1. They assume default probabilities are constant over the credit cycle
  2. They require estimates of asset value and asset volatility, which are not directly observable
  3. They provide no economic explanation for why default occurs
Answer
B is correct. The unobservability of firm asset value and its volatility is the classic structural-model criticism. C is the reduced-form criticism: it is precisely the structural model that does supply the economic story.
5. An analyst is evaluating a securitisation backed by a granular, homogeneous pool of consumer loans. Compared with a concentrated pool of a few large commercial loans, this pool is most likely to:
  1. require loan-by-loan analysis
  2. be suited to a statistical, portfolio-level approach
  3. carry no need to assess the servicer
Answer
B is correct. Granularity and homogeneity are exactly the conditions under which pool-level statistical analysis works. A describes the concentrated commercial pool. C is wrong in every securitisation: servicer quality and incentives matter regardless of pool composition.

LM5 · Credit Default Swaps

LOS: (a) CDS basics, single-name and index, and the defining parameters; (b) credit events and settlement; (c) pricing principles and the factors that drive them; (d) managing credit exposure and expressing curve views; (e) using CDS to exploit valuation disparities across markets.
Payout on a credit event
\[ \text{Payout ratio}=1-\text{Recovery rate},\qquad \text{Payout}=\text{Notional}\times(1-\text{RR}) \]
Upfront premium
\[ \text{Upfront premium}\approx(\text{CDS spread}-\text{CDS coupon})\times\text{EffSpreadDur}\times\text{Notional} \]
Standardised coupons are 1% for investment grade and 5% for high yield, so the difference between the market spread and the fixed coupon is settled up front.
Price of the CDS per 100 notional
\[ \text{Price}\approx 100-\text{Upfront premium \%} \]
Quoted like a bond price. Spread above coupon means the protection buyer pays up front, so the price is below 100.
Profit on unwinding at a new spread
\[ \%\ \Delta\text{Profit}\approx \Delta\text{Spread (bps)}\times\text{EffSpreadDur}\times\text{Notional} \]
Widening spreads profit the protection buyer. The sign convention costs marks: buyer of protection is short credit.
Basis trade
\[ \text{Basis}=\text{CDS spread}-\text{bond spread (Z-spread)} \]
Negative basis means the CDS is cheap relative to the bond: buy the bond and buy protection to capture the difference. Positive basis is the reverse.
Curve trade
Expecting the credit curve to steepen: buy long-dated protection, sell short-dated protection. Expecting flattening: the reverse. The trade is a view on the shape, largely neutral to a parallel move in the level.

What drives the spread
Two things, and only two, in the pricing framework: the probability of default and the expected recovery rate. Everything else - leverage, earnings volatility, the cycle, subordination - works through one of those. A higher hazard rate or a lower recovery rate means a wider spread.

Part 1 — What a CDS actually is (LOS a)

Insurance on a bond, structured as a swap. The protection buyer pays a fixed quarterly coupon on the notional and receives a payment if a credit event occurs. Contracts are standardised: fixed coupons of 1% or 5%, so the economic difference between the fair market spread and that fixed coupon is settled in a single upfront payment.

Single-name CDS reference one issuer. Index CDS (CDX, iTraxx) reference an equally weighted basket, are more liquid, and let you take a view on a whole credit segment. If a constituent defaults, it is removed from the index and settled individually; the index continues with the survivors at reduced notional.

Part 2 — Events and settlement (LOS b)

Three credit events: bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring. Note the regional split - restructuring is generally excluded from standard North American high-yield contracts and included in European contracts. ISDA's Determinations Committee decides whether an event occurred, which removes most of the litigation risk that plagued early contracts.

Cash settlement is now standard, using an auction to establish the recovery rate on the cheapest-to-deliver obligation. That "cheapest to deliver" wording matters: the payout is set by the cheapest qualifying bond, not by the one you happen to own.

Part 3 — Pricing (LOS c)

The spread compensates the protection seller for expected loss, so it rises with the hazard rate and falls with the expected recovery rate. In practice you quote the spread and convert to an upfront payment: if the market spread exceeds the fixed coupon the buyer pays up front, and if it is below the coupon the seller pays. The conversion uses the effective spread duration of the contract.

Mark to market: once the trade is on, a change in spreads produces gains or losses roughly equal to the spread change times the spread duration times the notional. The protection buyer gains when spreads widen.

Part 4 — Managing exposure and curve views (LOS d)

The direct use is hedging: a lender holding a loan buys protection to shed the credit risk without selling the asset, which preserves the client relationship. The synthetic use is the reverse: selling protection creates long credit exposure with no funding, useful when the cash bond is unavailable or expensive to buy.

Curve trades express a view on shape rather than level. If you expect the credit curve to steepen - short-term risk improving relative to long-term - buy long-dated protection and sell short-dated. Because the two legs offset on a parallel move, the position is largely a bet on the shape alone.

Part 5 — Valuation disparities (LOS e)

The basis trade is the classic. Same issuer, same maturity, two prices for the same credit risk: the bond's Z-spread and the CDS spread. When the basis is negative (CDS spread below bond spread) the protection is cheap, so buy the bond and buy protection and lock the difference. Positive basis reverses the trade.

The same logic extends across the capital structure and across markets: CDS against equity or equity options in a capital-structure arbitrage, or index CDS against the sum of its constituents when the index trades away from the intrinsic basket. All of these depend on the mispricing closing, and the risk is that it widens first.

Is buying protection the same as shorting the bond?
Economically similar, practically much easier. Shorting a corporate bond requires borrowing it, which is often impossible or expensive. A CDS gives the same directional credit exposure with a standardised contract, no bond borrow, and a known maximum cost - the coupon stream.
Why are contracts standardised at 1% and 5% coupons instead of just paying the market spread?
Standard coupons make contracts fungible, which lets positions be netted and unwound easily and supports central clearing. The spread-versus-coupon difference is handled by the upfront payment, so the economics are unchanged.
What does "cheapest to deliver" mean for the payout?
Several bonds can qualify as deliverable obligations. Since a rational protection buyer would deliver the cheapest one, the auction recovery rate is set by that bond, which makes the payout larger than it would be if the recovery on a higher-priced obligation were used. It is a real effect on expected payout, not a technicality.
If a name in a CDX index defaults, what happens to the index contract?
That name is settled separately, and the index continues with the remaining constituents at a proportionally reduced notional. The index does not terminate.
Why would a negative basis persist if it is free money?
It is not free. The trade requires funding the bond position, carries counterparty risk on the CDS, may face mark-to-market losses if the basis widens further, and can be constrained by balance-sheet limits. Basis trades blew up in 2008 for exactly these reasons.
1. A protection buyer holds a $10 million notional CDS. A credit event occurs and the auction sets the recovery rate at 35%. The payout to the protection buyer is closest to:
  1. $3.5 million
  2. $6.5 million
  3. $10.0 million
Answer
B is correct. \(10\text{m}\times(1-0.35)=6.5\text{m}\). A uses the recovery rate itself as the payout ratio.
2. An investment-grade CDS has a standard coupon of 1% and a market spread of 1.65%. The effective spread duration is 4.2. The upfront premium as a percentage of notional is closest to, and paid by:
  1. 2.73%, paid by the protection buyer
  2. 2.73%, paid by the protection seller
  3. 6.93%, paid by the protection buyer
Answer
A is correct. \((1.65\%-1.00\%)\times4.2=2.73\%\). Because the market spread exceeds the fixed coupon, the coupon alone under-compensates the seller, so the buyer pays the difference up front. C multiplies the full spread rather than the difference.
3. An analyst expects an issuer's near-term prospects to improve while its long-term outlook deteriorates. The most appropriate CDS curve trade is to:
  1. buy short-term protection and sell long-term protection
  2. sell short-term protection and buy long-term protection
  3. buy protection at all maturities
Answer
B is correct. That view is a steepening credit curve. Selling short-dated protection profits from near-term improvement, buying long-dated protection profits from long-term deterioration. A is the flattener. C is a view on the level, not the shape.
4. A five-year corporate bond trades at a Z-spread of 240 bps while the five-year CDS on the same issuer trades at 195 bps. The trade that most likely captures this disparity is to:
  1. buy the bond and buy CDS protection
  2. short the bond and sell CDS protection
  3. buy the bond and sell CDS protection
Answer
A is correct. The basis is \(195-240=-45\) bps, so protection is cheap relative to the bond. Buy the bond for 240 bps, pay 195 bps for protection, and keep roughly 45 bps with the credit risk largely hedged. C doubles the credit exposure instead of hedging it.
5. Which of the following is least likely to be a credit event under a standard North American high-yield CDS contract?
  1. Bankruptcy
  2. Failure to pay
  3. Restructuring
Answer
C is correct. Restructuring is generally excluded from standard North American high-yield contracts, though it is included in European contracts. Bankruptcy and failure to pay are credit events everywhere.
