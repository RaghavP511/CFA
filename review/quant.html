LM1 · Basics of Multiple Regression & Underlying Assumptions

LOS: (a) problems addressed by MLR and the regression process; (b) formulate a model and interpret slope coefficients; (c) explain assumptions and interpret residual plots for violations.
Multiple linear regression model
\[ Y_i = b_0 + b_1 X_{1i} + b_2 X_{2i} + \cdots + b_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i \]
\(b_0\)=intercept; \(b_1\ldots b_k\)=partial slope coefficients; \(\varepsilon_i\)=error; \(i=1,\ldots,n\).
Predicted (fitted) value
\[ \hat{Y}_i = \hat{b}_0 + \hat{b}_1 X_{1i} + \cdots + \hat{b}_k X_{ki} \]
The deterministic part with estimated coefficients.
Partial slope coefficient (interpretation)
\[ b_j = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial X_j}\quad\text{(all other IVs held constant)} \]
Expected change in DV per one-unit change in \(X_j\), holding all other IVs fixed.
Residual (in-sample error)
\[ e_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i \]
Estimate of the unobservable error \(\varepsilon_i\).
Homoskedasticity condition (Assumption 2)
\[ \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid X) = \sigma^2 \quad(\text{constant for all } i) \]
Error variance the same across all observations.
Dummy variables for a qualitative IV
\[ \text{number of dummies} = m - 1 \]
\(m\)=categories; \(m-1\) avoids the dummy-variable trap.

Part 1 — What Multiple Regression Does (LOS a)

MLR extends simple regression to two or more independent variables, serving three purposes:

  • Identify relationships between variables.
  • Test existing theories.
  • Forecast a dependent variable.

The regression process is iterative: specify → estimate → analyze residuals/fit → revise if assumptions are violated.

Part 2 — Formulating & Interpreting (LOS b)

2.1 The model
\[ Y_i = b_0 + b_1 X_{1i} + \cdots + b_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n \]

Two pieces: the deterministic part \(b_0 + b_1X_{1i}+\cdots\) (explained) and the stochastic part \(\varepsilon_i\) (unexplained).

2.2 Interpreting a partial slope coefficient
\[ b_j = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial X_j}\quad\text{(all other IVs held constant)} \]

Change in the DV per one-unit change in that IV, holding all others constant — the phrase examiners look for.

2.3 Worked interpretation
\[ \text{RET} = 0.0023 - 5.0585\,(\text{BY}) - 2.1901\,(\text{CS}) \]
  • Intercept: both IVs = 0 → RET = 0.0023.
  • BY: +1 in bond yield lowers RET by 5.0585, holding CS constant.
  • CS: −1 in credit spread raises RET by 2.1901, holding BY constant.
2.4 Dummy variables
\[ \text{number of dummies} = m - 1 \]

Using \(m-1\) dummies avoids the dummy-variable trap; the omitted category becomes the base (reference) category.

Part 3 — The Five Assumptions (LOS c)

#AssumptionMeaning
1LinearityDV–IV relationship linear (in coefficients).
2HomoskedasticityConstant error variance: \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i\mid X)=\sigma^2\).
3Independence of errorsResiduals uncorrelated across observations.
4NormalityResiduals normally distributed.
5Independence of IVsIVs non-random; no exact linear relationship among them.
Assumption 2 fails → heteroskedasticity; 3 fails → serial correlation; 5 fails → multicollinearity. All three are the heart of LM3.

Part 4 — Diagnostic Plots

4.1 Scatterplot matrix

Want linearity between DV and IVs; do not want strong relationships among IVs (multicollinearity warning).

4.2 Residual scatterplot

Good: random, constant spread. Bad: fanning (heteroskedasticity) or a directional pattern (correlated errors / misspecification).

4.3 Q–Q plot

On the straight line → normal residuals; tails peeling off → outliers/non-normality.

Exam-focus cheat sheet

\[ Y_i = b_0 + b_1 X_{1i} + \cdots + b_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i \]
  • Partial slope = ΔDV per one-unit ΔIV, all else constant.
  • Qualitative IV with \(m\) categories → \(m-1\) dummies; omitted = base.
  • Five assumptions: Linearity · Homoskedasticity · Independent errors · Normality · Independent IVs.
  • Scatterplot matrix: want DV–IV linearity, not IV–IV.
  • Q–Q plot: on the line = normal.

LM2 · Evaluating Regression Model Fit & Interpreting Results

LOS: (a) evaluate fit via the ANOVA table and goodness-of-fit measures; (b) hypotheses on two+ coefficients and joint (F) tests; (c) calculate and interpret a predicted value.
Sum-of-squares identity
\[ \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2}_{\text{SST}} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{Y}_i-\bar{Y})^2}_{\text{SSR}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\hat{Y}_i)^2}_{\text{SSE}} \]
Total = Explained (regression) + Unexplained (error).
Coefficient of determination
\[ R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} \]
Fraction of DV variation explained; never decreases when IVs are added.
Adjusted \(R^2\) — three equivalent forms
\[ \bar{R}^2 = 1 - \left(\frac{\text{SSE}/(n-k-1)}{\text{SST}/(n-1)}\right) = 1 - \left(\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}\right)(1-R^2) = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}} \]
Penalizes for df. Rises only if added coefficient has \(|t|>1.0\); can be negative.
Caution — MST − MSE, not MSR: \(\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}} = \dfrac{\text{MST}-\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}}\), which does not equal \(\text{MSR}/\text{MST}\) — mean squares use different df, so \(\text{MST}\neq\text{MSR}+\text{MSE}\).
Mean squares (each = SS ÷ its own df)
\[ \text{MSR}=\frac{\text{SSR}}{k},\quad \text{MSE}=\frac{\text{SSE}}{n-k-1},\quad \text{MST}=\frac{\text{SST}}{n-1} \]
df add up: \(k+(n-k-1)=n-1\). MST is the sample variance of \(Y\).
Standard error of the estimate
\[ \text{SEE}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}}=\sqrt{\frac{\text{SSE}}{n-k-1}} \]
Typical size of the residuals; lower is better.
General linear F-test (whole model)
\[ F=\frac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}}=\frac{\text{SSR}/k}{\text{SSE}/(n-k-1)},\quad df=(k,\,n-k-1) \]
\(H_0: b_1=\cdots=b_k=0\). One-tailed right. Reject if \(F>F_{\text{crit}}\).
Nested (partial) F-test
\[ F=\frac{(\text{SSE}_R-\text{SSE}_U)/q}{\text{SSE}_U/(n-k-1)},\quad df=(q,\,n-k-1) \]
\(q\)=# restrictions, \(k\)=IVs in unrestricted model. Collapses to general F when \(q=k\).
t-test for a single coefficient
\[ t=\frac{\hat{b}_j - B_j}{s_{\hat{b}_j}},\quad df=n-k-1 \]
Usually \(B_j=0\). Identical to simple-regression t-test.
AIC & BIC
\[ \text{AIC}=n\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}\right)+2(k+1) \] \[ \text{BIC}=n\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}\right)+\ln(n)\,(k+1) \]
Lower = better. AIC → prediction; BIC → goodness of fit. BIC penalizes complexity more (\(\ln n>2\)).
Prediction & forecast interval
\[ \hat{Y}=\hat{b}_0+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{b}_j X_j,\qquad \hat{Y}\pm t_{\text{crit}}\cdot s_f \]
Use all coefficients. \(s_f>\text{SEE}\) because IV values are themselves estimated.

\(R^2\)% of DV variation explained; flaw: never falls as IVs are added.
Adjusted \(R^2\)\(R^2\) penalized for df; the right fit measure in MLR.
\(|t|>1.0\) ruleAdding a variable raises \(\bar{R}^2\) only if its \(|t|>1\) — below significance.

MSEUnexplained variance per residual df; \(\text{SSE}/(n-k-1)\).
General F-testWhole-model explanatory power; \(F=\text{MSR}/\text{MSE}\).

Part 1 — Goodness of Fit (LOS a)

1.1 Sum-of-squares foundation
\[ \text{SST}=\text{SSR}+\text{SSE} \]
  • SST — total dispersion of \(Y\) around its mean.
  • SSR — explained (predicted around the mean).
  • SSE — unexplained (actual around predicted).
1.2 Coefficient of determination
\[ R^2=\frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}}=1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} \]

Critical flaw: \(R^2\) never decreases as IVs are added — adding an IV can only lower SSE.

Overfitting — too complex; coefficients stop representing true relationships. Symptom: low in-sample error, high out-of-sample error.
1.3 Adjusted \(R^2\)
\[ \bar{R}^2 = 1 - \left(\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}\right)(1-R^2) = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}} \]
  • If \(k\geq1\), \(\bar{R}^2
  • Adding a variable: \(\bar{R}^2\) rises if \(|t|>1.0\), falls if \(|t|<1.0\).
Caution: \(\bar{R}^2=1-\dfrac{\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}}=\dfrac{\text{MST}-\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}}\neq\dfrac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MST}}\) — mean squares use different df.
1.4 Mean squares & degrees of freedom
\[ \text{MSR}=\frac{\text{SSR}}{k},\quad \text{MSE}=\frac{\text{SSE}}{n-k-1},\quad \text{MST}=\frac{\text{SST}}{n-1} \] \[ \underbrace{k}_{\text{reg}}+\underbrace{(n-k-1)}_{\text{error}}=\underbrace{n-1}_{\text{total}} \] \[ \text{SEE}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}} \]
1.5 AIC and BIC
\[ \text{AIC}=n\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}\right)+2(k+1),\qquad \text{BIC}=n\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}\right)+\ln(n)(k+1) \]

Lower = better. AIC → prediction; BIC → goodness of fit. BIC penalizes complexity more. Absolute values meaningless — only relative matters.

Part 2 — Hypothesis Testing (LOS b)

2.1 Single coefficient — t-test
\[ t=\frac{\hat{b}_j-B_j}{s_{\hat{b}_j}},\quad df=n-k-1 \]
2.2 Nested (restricted) F-test
\[ F=\frac{(\text{SSE}_R-\text{SSE}_U)/q}{\text{SSE}_U/(n-k-1)},\quad df=(q,\,n-k-1) \]
2.3 General linear F-test
\[ H_0: b_1=\cdots=b_k=0,\qquad F=\frac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}},\quad df=(k,\,n-k-1) \] \[ F=\frac{18.1247}{1.2868}=14.085 \]

At 5% (right-tailed), \(F_{5,44}=2.427\). Since \(14.085>2.427\), reject \(H_0\) — the model has explanatory power.

Trap: a significant F with insignificant individual t-stats is the classic multicollinearity signature (LM3).

Part 3 — Prediction (LOS c)

\[ \hat{Y}=\hat{b}_0+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{b}_j X_j \]

Rule: use all coefficients, even insignificant ones.

\[ \text{RET}=-0.823+1.719(3)+0.412(-2)+0.026(-5)+0.553(1)-0.067(3)=3.732 \]

The forecast standard error \(s_f>\text{SEE}\) because you also had to forecast the IV values (model error + sampling error).

LM3 · Model Misspecification

LOS: (a) misspecification effects and avoidance; (b) heteroskedasticity; (c) serial correlation; (d) multicollinearity.
Omitted-variable bias condition
\[ \operatorname{Corr}(X_{\text{omitted}},X_{\text{included}})\neq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{b}\ \text{biased \& inconsistent} \]
If uncorrelated with included IVs, coefficients stay unbiased but errors may become CH/SC.
Breusch–Pagan — conditional heteroskedasticity
\[ \text{BP}=n\cdot R^2_{\text{resid}}\;\sim\;\chi^2_k \]
Regress squared residuals on IVs. \(H_0\): no CH. Right-tailed, \(df=k\).
Durbin–Watson — first-order serial correlation
\[ \text{DW}\approx 2(1-r) \]
\(r\)=residual autocorrelation. DW=2 → none; <2 → positive SC; >2 → negative SC.
Variance inflation factor — multicollinearity
\[ \text{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R^2_j} \]
\(R^2_j\) from regressing \(X_j\) on other IVs. Min = 1; >5 concerning; >10 serious.
Well-specified model: economic reasoning · parsimonious · good out-of-sample · appropriate functional form · satisfies assumptions.

Part 1 — Specification Principles (LOS a)

A good model is grounded in economic reasoning, parsimonious, performs well out-of-sample, has appropriate functional form, and satisfies the assumptions.

FailureConsequence
Omitted variablesIf correlated with included IVs → biased/inconsistent coefficients; else errors may show CH/SC.
Wrong functional formIgnoring non-linearity; transform variables; possible CH.
Inappropriate scalingPossible heteroskedasticity or multicollinearity.
Inappropriate poolingMixing populations/regimes; possible CH and SC.
\[ \operatorname{Corr}(X_{\text{omitted}},X_{\text{included}})\neq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{b}\ \text{biased and inconsistent} \]

Part 2 — Heteroskedasticity (LOS b)

Definition: non-constant error variance (violates Assumption 2).

  • Unconditional: unrelated to IVs → no inference problem.
  • Conditional (CH): correlated with IVs → the damaging type.

Effects of CH: coefficients still consistent; SEs biased (usually underestimated) → t-stats inflated → Type I error; F-test unreliable.

Test — Breusch–Pagan
\[ \text{BP}=n\cdot R^2_{\text{resid}}\;\sim\;\chi^2_k \]

Regress squared residuals on the IVs. \(H_0\): no CH. Right-tailed, \(df=k\).

Fix

Robust / White / heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors. Coefficients unchanged; only SEs corrected.

Part 3 — Serial Correlation (LOS c)

Definition: errors correlated across observations (violates Assumption 3). Common in time series.

  • Positive SC: positive residual followed by positive (most common).
  • Negative SC: positive followed by negative.

Effects: coefficients consistent — unless a lagged DV is an IV (then invalid). Positive SC underestimates SEs → t and F inflated → Type I error.

Tests
\[ \text{DW}\approx 2(1-r) \]

DW tests first-order only (DW=2 none; <2 positive; >2 negative). Breusch–Godfrey is more general (any lag).

Fix

Newey–West (serial-correlation-consistent) SEs — also correct for CH.

Part 4 — Multicollinearity (LOS d)

Definition: two+ IVs highly correlated / approximate linear relationship among IVs (violates Assumption 5).

Effects: coefficients consistent but imprecise; SEs inflated → insignificant t-stats, yet a significant F and high \(R^2\). That contradiction is the tell.

Detection — VIF
\[ \text{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R^2_j} \]

Min = 1; >5 concerning; >10 serious.

Fix

Drop a correlated IV (preferred); use a different proxy; increase sample size.

If the goal is pure prediction, a significant model matters more than significant coefficients, so multicollinearity is less of a concern.

LM4 · Extensions of Multiple Regression

LOS: (a) influence analysis and detecting influential data points; (b) qualitative independent variables (dummies); (c) formulate and interpret a logistic regression model.
Leverage measure — high-leverage threshold
\[ h_{ii} > 3\left(\frac{k+1}{n}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{potentially influential} \]
\(h_{ii}\) ranges 0–1; leverages sum to \(k+1\). Large samples use the tighter \(2\left(\frac{k+1}{n}\right)\) (Belsley, Kuh, Welsch 1980).
Studentized residual — outlier detection
\[ t_i^* = \frac{e_i^*}{s_{e^*}} \;\sim\; t_{\,n-k-2} \]
Delete obs. \(i\), refit on \(n-1\) cases. Flag if \(|t_i^*|>\) critical t (small samples) or \(|t_i^*|>3\) (large samples).
Dummy variable forms
\[ Y=b_0+b_1X+d_1D \quad\text{(intercept)} \] \[ Y=b_0+b_1X+d_1(D\cdot X) \quad\text{(slope / interaction)} \] \[ Y=b_0+b_1X+d_1D+d_2(D\cdot X) \quad\text{(both)} \]
\(D=0\) gives the base line; \(D=1\) shifts intercept and/or slope. Use \(m-1\) dummies for \(m\) categories.
Odds & logit (log odds)
\[ \text{Odds}=\frac{P}{1-P}, \qquad \ln\!\left(\frac{P}{1-P}\right)=b_0+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_kX_k+\varepsilon \]
e.g. \(P=0.75 \Rightarrow\) odds \(=3\) (3 to 1). The log odds is the logit.
Logistic function (recover probability)
\[ P=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left[-(b_0+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_kX_k)\right]} \]
Sigmoidal, bounded 0–1. \(P\to1\) as linear term \(\to+\infty\); \(P\to0\) as \(\to-\infty\).
Odds ratio
\[ \text{Odds ratio}=e^{b_j} \]
Ratio of odds with a one-unit increase in \(X_j\) to odds without it. Odds ratios move exponentially with ΔIV.
Likelihood ratio (LR) test — model fit
\[ \text{LR}=-2\left(\text{LL}_{\text{restricted}}-\text{LL}_{\text{unrestricted}}\right)\;\sim\;\chi^2_{\,q} \]
\(q\)=# restrictions. \(H_0\): restricted model is better. Log-likelihood always negative; closer to 0 = better fit.

LM4 stretches the toolkit three ways: unusual observations, qualitative inputs, and qualitative outputs.

Part 1 — Influence Analysis (LOS a)

1.1 Two kinds of unusual observations
TypeWhat's extreme
High-leverage pointAn extreme value of an independent variable (X).
OutlierAn extreme value of the dependent variable (Y) — a large residual.

A point can be one, the other, or both. An extreme point that lies on the regression line may not be a problem — being unusual is not the same as being influential. An influential observation is one whose removal would materially change the coefficients.

1.2 Detecting high leverage — \(h_{ii}\)

Leverage \(h_{ii}\) measures how far obs. \(i\)'s X-values sit from the mean; it ranges 0–1 and leverages sum to \(k+1\).

\[ h_{ii} > 3\left(\frac{k+1}{n}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{potentially influential} \]

Large samples use the tighter \(2\left(\frac{k+1}{n}\right)\) (Belsley, Kuh, Welsch 1980). Example: \(n=96,\ k=2\Rightarrow 3(3/96)=0.09375\) — anything above is flagged.

1.3 Detecting outliers — studentized residuals

Delete obs. \(i\), refit on the remaining \(n-1\), and compute:

\[ t_i^* = \frac{e_i^*}{s_{e^*}} \;\sim\; t_{\,n-k-2} \]

Flag if \(|t_i^*|>\) critical t (small samples, preferred) or \(|t_i^*|>3\) (large samples).

Part 2 — Qualitative Independent Variables (LOS b)

A dummy takes value 0 or 1. Use \(m-1\) dummies for \(m\) categories; the omitted category is the base captured by the intercept.

Three ways a dummy enters
\[ Y=b_0+b_1X+d_1D \quad\text{(intercept dummy — parallel shift)} \] \[ Y=b_0+b_1X+d_1(D\cdot X) \quad\text{(slope dummy — interaction)} \] \[ Y=b_0+b_1X+d_1D+d_2(D\cdot X) \quad\text{(both)} \]

With "both": \(D=0\Rightarrow Y=b_0+b_1X\); \(D=1\Rightarrow Y=(b_0+d_1)+(b_1+d_2)X\) — different intercept and slope. Significance is assessed with individual t-tests on each dummy.

Part 3 — Logistic Regression (LOS c)

3.1 Why not OLS?

With a binary DV (fraud/no-fraud, bankrupt/solvent), a linear probability model can predict \(P<0\) or \(P>1\) and the relationship isn't linear.

3.2 Odds, log odds, logit
\[ \text{Odds}=\frac{P}{1-P}\qquad\left(P=0.75\Rightarrow\frac{0.75}{0.25}=3,\ \text{i.e. 3 to 1}\right) \] \[ \ln\!\left(\frac{P}{1-P}\right)=b_0+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_kX_k+\varepsilon \] \[ P=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left[-(b_0+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_kX_k)\right]} \]

The curve is sigmoidal, bounded 0–1; the error follows a logistic distribution (fatter tails than normal).

3.3 Estimation — MLE

Coefficients are estimated by maximum likelihood (not least squares), choosing values that make the observed outcomes most likely; the likelihood uses the binomial distribution.

3.4 Interpreting coefficients
QuantityInterpretation
Slope \(b_j\)Change in the log odds per one-unit change in \(X_j\), holding all other IVs constant.
Odds ratio\(e^{b_j}\) — odds with a one-unit increase vs. without.

As an IV changes: log odds move linearly, odds ratios exponentially, probabilities non-linearly (marginal effect largest near \(P=0.5\)).

3.5 Goodness of fit — the LR test

No \(R^2\); software reports a pseudo-\(R^2\), usable only to compare same-DV specifications on the same data. For significance:

\[ \text{LR}=-2\left(\text{LL}_{\text{restricted}}-\text{LL}_{\text{unrestricted}}\right)\;\sim\;\chi^2_{\,q} \]

\(q\)=# restrictions; \(H_0\): the restricted model is better; rejecting favors the unrestricted model. Log-likelihood is always negative (closer to 0 = better), meaningful only relative to another same-DV model. Best in large samples.

Exam-focus cheat sheet

\[ h_{ii}>3\left(\frac{k+1}{n}\right);\qquad t_i^*\sim t_{\,n-k-2},\ |t_i^*|>3 \text{ (large)} \] \[ \ln\!\left(\frac{P}{1-P}\right)=b_0+\sum b_jX_j;\qquad P=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(b_0+\sum b_jX_j)]} \] \[ \text{LR}=-2(\text{LL}_R-\text{LL}_U)\sim\chi^2_{\,q} \]
  • Leverage flags extreme X; studentized residual flags extreme Y.
  • Dummies: intercept = shift; slope (interaction) = tilt; use \(m-1\).
  • Logistic: binary DV → model log odds; estimate by MLE; slope = Δ log odds per unit ΔIV, all else constant; odds ratio \(=e^{b_j}\).
  • Fit: pseudo-\(R^2\) (same model) and the LR test (\(\chi^2\), \(q\) df).

★ Statistical Tests Reference — t vs. F vs. Chi-Square

Cross-cutting reference across LM2 (t & F), LM3 (Breusch–Pagan \(\chi^2\)), LM4 (logistic LR test), and later time-series (Dickey–Fuller, ARCH).
TestUse it for
t-testA single parameter — one mean or one coefficient.
F-testMultiple parameters jointly, or a ratio of two variances.
Chi-square \(\chi^2\)A single variance, counts/proportions, or spec tests (Breusch–Pagan, logistic LR).
Why they differ: a \(t\) is a normal divided by a scaled chi-square; an \(F\) is a ratio of two chi-squares. Relatives, but different questions.

t-test — one coefficient / mean
\[ t=\frac{\hat{b}_j-B_j}{s_{\hat{b}_j}},\quad df=n-k-1 \]
One restriction. One- or two-tailed. Variance unknown.
F-test — joint significance of coefficients
\[ F=\frac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}}=\frac{\text{SSR}/k}{\text{SSE}/(n-k-1)},\quad df=(k,\,n-k-1) \]
\(H_0: b_1=\cdots=b_k=0\). Right-tailed.
F-test — equality of two variances
\[ F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2},\quad df=(n_1-1,\,n_2-1) \]
Ratio of two sample variances (larger over smaller). Right-tailed.
Chi-square — single variance
\[ \chi^2=\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2},\quad df=n-1 \]
Tests one variance vs. hypothesized \(\sigma_0^2\). Right-tailed.
Chi-square — goodness of fit / independence
\[ \chi^2=\sum \frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i} \]
Observed vs. expected frequencies for categorical data.
Chi-square — Breusch–Pagan (spec test)
\[ \text{BP}=n\cdot R^2_{\text{resid}}\;\sim\;\chi^2_k \]
Conditional heteroskedasticity test (LM3). Right-tailed, \(df=k\).
Chi-square — logistic likelihood ratio (LR)
\[ \text{LR}=-2(\text{LL}_R-\text{LL}_U)\;\sim\;\chi^2_{\,q} \]
Logistic-regression model fit (LM4). \(q\)=# restrictions. Right-tailed.
